How these dates are reckoned

Dates follow {{ source_name }} and are computed for New Delhi, IST. A tithi begins and ends at a clock instant, not at midnight — so another city, especially outside India, can legitimately land on the adjacent day.

Month names use the purnimanta (North Indian) reckoning, in which the lunar month ends at the full moon. An amanta (South and West Indian) calendar names the same day under a different month.

Several festivals are fixed by the tithi prevailing at a particular window — pradosh, nishita, madhyahna, aparahna, or moonrise — rather than at sunrise, and regional and family traditions genuinely observe some of them a day either side. Where your family or local priest keeps a different day, that day is not wrong.

Where this calendar carries the recurring lunar days — Purnima, Amavasya and Ekadashi — they are reckoned from the tithi prevailing at sunrise in New Delhi. Where one tithi covers two sunrises (vriddhi) there are two candidate days: for Purnima and Amavasya we list the first, and for Ekadashi the second, since a tithi that long is already pierced by the preceding Dashami at the first sunrise. That is the Smarta convention; the Vaishnava (ISKCON) Ekadashi is reckoned separately and can fall a day later again, and legitimately so. Where a tithi begins and ends between two sunrises (kshaya) we take the day it begins on. A festival fixed by a dusk or midnight window can therefore fall the day before the Purnima or Amavasya it belongs to: Diwali 2026 is listed here on 8 November, while the Kartika Amavasya reckoned at sunrise is 9 November — Diwali is set by the pradosh (dusk) window, not by sunrise.
